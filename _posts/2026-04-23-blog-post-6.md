I recently took a course on "Power System Stability and Control," and an interesting question suddenly struck me. This post studies this small but important question in a single-machine infinite-bus (SMIB) system:

> What changes when the excitation system is changed from proportional voltage feedback to PI voltage feedback?

The short answer is that adding integral action forces the terminal voltage to track the reference value at steady state. However, this improvement comes with a price: the PI controller introduces an additional slow pole. In this example, that slow pole mainly affects the excitation and voltage-related states, such as the integral state, excitation voltage, internal voltage, and terminal voltage. It is not mainly a frequency oscillation mode, although the frequency state can still be weakly affected through system coupling.

The analysis below compares two cases:

1. AVR with proportional voltage feedback only.
2. AVR with PI voltage feedback.

The comparison is based on nonlinear equilibrium calculation, small-signal linearization, eigenvalue analysis, and the transfer function from the excitation input $u$ to the rotor speed deviation $\omega$.

---

## 1. Model Setup

Before adding integral action, the state vector is

$$
x=
\begin{bmatrix}
E_{fd} \\
e \\
\delta \\
\omega
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Here:

- $E_{fd}$ is the excitation voltage state.
- $e$ is the internal transient voltage magnitude.
- $\delta$ is the rotor angle.
- $\omega$ is the rotor speed deviation.

The terminal voltage magnitude is

$$
V=
\sqrt{
\alpha^2 e^2
+2\alpha\beta e\cos\delta
+\beta^2
}.
$$

The constants $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are defined as

$$
\alpha=\frac{x_L}{x+x_L},
\qquad
\beta=\frac{x}{x+x_L}.
$$

The network angle is

$$
\theta
=
\tan^{-1}\left(
\frac{\alpha e\sin\delta}{\alpha e\cos\delta+\beta}
\right).
$$

In numerical implementation, it is usually better to compute this angle with `atan2` rather than `atan`, because `atan2` handles the quadrant correctly. The expression above matches the mathematical form used in the simulation.

The machine and excitation dynamics are

$$
\dot E_{fd}
=
\frac{1}{T'_{d0}}
\left(
-K_1E_{fd}
-K_2(V-V^\star)
+u
\right),
$$

$$
\dot e
=
\frac{1}{T_d}
\left(
-ae
+bV\cos(\delta-\theta)
+E_{fd}
\right),
$$

$$
\dot\delta=\omega,
$$

$$
\dot\omega
=
\frac{1}{M}
\left(
P_m
-d\omega
-\frac{eV\sin(\delta-\theta)}{x}
\right).
$$

The constants in the transient voltage equation are

$$
a=\frac{x_d}{x'_d},
\qquad
b=\frac{x_d-x'_d}{x'_d}.
$$

The parameter values used in the simulation are

$$
T'_{d0}=0.1,
\qquad
T_d=0.05,
\qquad
K_1=1.5,
\qquad
K_2=6,
$$

$$
M=0.2,
\qquad
d=0.28,
\qquad
P_m=0.1,
\qquad
V^\star=0.9.
$$

## 2. Excitation System Without PI Control

Without integral action, the excitation equation is

$$
\dot E_{fd}
=
\frac{1}{T'_{d0}}
\left(
-K_1E_{fd}
-K_2(V-V^\star)
+u
\right).
$$

For the equilibrium calculation in this post, take $u=0$. At equilibrium,

$$
\dot E_{fd}=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
0=-K_1E_{fd}-K_2(V-V^\star).
$$

Solving this equation gives

$$
K_1E_{fd}=-K_2(V-V^\star),
$$

or equivalently,

$$
E_{fd}
=-\frac{K_2}{K_1}(V-V^\star)
=
\frac{K_2}{K_1}(V^\star-V).
$$

This equation is the key point. It does **not** require

$$
V=V^\star.
$$

Instead, the excitation voltage $E_{fd}$ balances the proportional voltage error. Therefore, a proportional AVR can stabilize the voltage, but it generally leaves a nonzero steady-state voltage error.

The computed equilibrium is

$$
x_e^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
E_{fd,e} \\
e_e \\
\delta_e \\
\omega_e
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.361147998565988 \\
0.678486721654244 \\
0.648869391580794 \\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The corresponding terminal voltage is

$$
V_e^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}=0.809713000359.
$$

Since

$$
V^\star=0.9,
$$

the steady-state voltage error is

$$
V_e^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}-V^\star
=0.809713000359-0.9
=-0.090286999641.
$$

Thus, the proportional AVR stabilizes the system, but the voltage settles below the reference value.

## 3. Adding PI Control to the Excitation System

Now introduce an integral state $W_1$ with dynamics

$$
\dot W_1=V-V^\star.
$$

The excitation equation becomes

$$
\dot E_{fd}
=
\frac{1}{T'_{d0}}
\left(
-K_1E_{fd}
-K_2(V-V^\star)
-K_3W_1
+u
\right).
$$

The new state vector is

$$
x=
\begin{bmatrix}
E_{fd} \\
e \\
\delta \\
\omega \\
W_1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The key difference comes from the equilibrium condition of the integral state. At equilibrium,

$$
\dot W_1=0.
$$

Because

$$
\dot W_1=V-V^\star,
$$

we must have

$$
V_e-V^\star=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
V_e=V^\star.
$$

This is the mathematical reason why integral action removes the steady-state voltage error.

The computed equilibrium after adding PI control is

$$
x_e^{\mathrm{PI}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
E_{fd,e} \\
e_e \\
\delta_e \\
\omega_e \\
W_{1,e}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.770015681067331 \\
0.846375002432449 \\
0.5056987424032 \\
0 \\
-2.31004704320199
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The corresponding terminal voltage is

$$
V_e^{\mathrm{PI}}=0.900000000000.
$$

Thus, after adding PI control,

$$
V_e^{\mathrm{PI}}=V^\star.
$$

The operating point changes significantly. The excitation voltage increases from

$$
E_{fd,e}^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}=0.3611
$$

to

$$
E_{fd,e}^{\mathrm{PI}}=0.7700.
$$

The internal voltage increases from

$$
e_e^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}=0.6785
$$

to

$$
e_e^{\mathrm{PI}}=0.8464.
$$

The rotor angle decreases from

$$
\delta_e^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}=0.6489
$$

to

$$
\delta_e^{\mathrm{PI}}=0.5057.
$$

This means the PI excitation controller not only removes the voltage steady-state error, but also changes the equilibrium of the electromechanical system.

## 4. Linearization Method

For small-signal analysis, write the nonlinear model as

$$
\dot x=f(x,u).
$$

Linearizing around an equilibrium point $(x_e,u_e)$ gives

$$
\Delta\dot x=A\Delta x+B\Delta u,
$$

where

$$
A=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{x=x_e,u=u_e},
\qquad
B=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\right|_{x=x_e,u=u_e}.
$$

The terminal voltage small-signal output is

$$
\Delta V=C_V\Delta x,
$$

where

$$
C_V=\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\right|_{x=x_e}.
$$

Since $V$ depends only on $e$ and $\delta$, the no-PI case has

$$
C_V^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \dfrac{\partial V}{\partial e} & \dfrac{\partial V}{\partial \delta} & 0
\end{bmatrix},
$$

and the PI case has

$$
C_V^{\mathrm{PI}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \dfrac{\partial V}{\partial e} & \dfrac{\partial V}{\partial \delta} & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

From

$$
V=
\sqrt{
\alpha^2 e^2
+2\alpha\beta e\cos\delta
+\beta^2
},
$$

we obtain

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial e}
=
\frac{\alpha^2e+\alpha\beta\cos\delta}{V},
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial \delta}
=
\frac{-\alpha\beta e\sin\delta}{V}.
$$

The frequency output is

$$
\Delta\omega=C_\omega\Delta x.
$$

For the no-PI model,

$$
C_\omega^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&0&0&1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For the PI model,

$$
C_\omega^{\mathrm{PI}}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&0&0&1&0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

## 5. Linearized Model Without PI

The linearized state-space model without PI control is

$$
\Delta\dot x=A_{\mathrm{no\ PI}}\Delta x+B_{\mathrm{no\ PI}}\Delta u.
$$

The numerical matrices are shown below.

```text
A_noPI =
   -14.9999999993211   -25.4781057551501     7.55461894375031    0
    19.9999999994649   -73.6585365856968   -32.4251002936649     0
     0                   0                   0                   1
     0                  -0.736934097599062  -0.659263636008933  -1.40000000002638

B_noPI =
    10
     0
     0
     0

C_V_noPI =
     0    0.424635095919168   -0.125910315729172    0

C_w_noPI =
     0    0    0    1
```

The eigenvalues are

$$
\lambda(A_{\mathrm{no\ PI}})
=
\left\{
-63.0461,
-25.6195,
-0.6965+j0.1446,
-0.6965-j0.1446
\right\}.
$$

All eigenvalues are in the open left-half plane, so the no-PI equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable.

The two fast real poles are

$$
-63.0461,
\qquad
-25.6195.
$$

These poles are mainly associated with the fast excitation and internal voltage dynamics.

The complex pair is

$$
-0.6965\pm j0.1446.
$$

This is the dominant electromechanical mode.

For a complex pair

$$
s=-\sigma\pm j\omega_d,
$$

the natural frequency and damping ratio are

$$
\omega_n=\sqrt{\sigma^2+\omega_d^2},
\qquad
\zeta=\frac{\sigma}{\omega_n}.
$$

For the no-PI case,

$$
\sigma=0.6965,
\qquad
\omega_d=0.1446.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\omega_n
\approx
\sqrt{0.6965^2+0.1446^2}
\approx 0.7113,
$$

and

$$
\zeta
\approx
\frac{0.6965}{0.7113}
\approx 0.979.
$$

Thus, the no-PI electromechanical mode is highly damped and only weakly oscillatory.

## 6. Linearized Model With PI

After adding PI control, the linearized model becomes

$$
\Delta\dot x=A_{\mathrm{PI}}\Delta x+B_{\mathrm{PI}}\Delta u.
$$

The numerical matrices are

```text
A_PI =
   -14.9999999998762   -26.6199845000425     6.79674796777441    0   -5.00000000069889
    20.0000000005751   -73.6585365856968   -25.9932062451895     0    0
     0                   0                   0                   1    0
     0                  -0.590754687494754  -0.9029749930764    -1.40000000002638  0
     0                   0.443666408334042  -0.11327913279624    0    0

B_PI =
    10
     0
     0
     0
     0

C_V_PI =
     0    0.443666408334042   -0.11327913279624    0    0

C_w_PI =
     0    0    0    1    0
```

The eigenvalues are

$$
\lambda(A_{\mathrm{PI}})
=
\left\{
-62.4484,
-26.1878,
-0.6933+j0.5644,
-0.6933-j0.5644,
-0.0358
\right\}.
$$

Again, all eigenvalues are in the open left-half plane, so the PI-controlled equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable.

However, a new pole appears:

$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{slow}}=-0.0358.
$$

This pole is close to the imaginary axis. Its time constant is approximately

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}
=\frac{1}{0.0358}
\approx 27.9\ \mathrm{s}.
$$

A rough four-time-constant settling estimate is

$$
T_s\approx 4\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}
\approx 111.7\ \mathrm{s}.
$$

This explains why PI control can remove the voltage steady-state error, but the final convergence of some variables becomes slow.

The electromechanical pair becomes

$$
-0.6933\pm j0.5644.
$$

For this mode,

$$
\sigma=0.6933,
\qquad
\omega_d=0.5644.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\omega_n
=
\sqrt{0.6933^2+0.5644^2}
\approx 0.894,
$$

and

$$
\zeta
=\frac{0.6933}{0.894}
\approx 0.776.
$$

Compared with the no-PI case, the damping ratio of the electromechanical mode decreases from approximately

$$
0.979
$$

to

$$
0.776.
$$

However, the real part of the electromechanical pair remains close to $-0.69$. Therefore, the main new slow behavior is not caused by the electromechanical complex pair. It is caused by the new real pole near the origin:

$$
-0.0358.
$$

## 7. Which State Becomes Slow After Adding PI?

The PI controller adds the integral state

$$
W_1.
$$

Its dynamics are

$$
\dot W_1=V-V^\star.
$$

Therefore, the new slow pole is most directly associated with the integral state $W_1$.

Since $W_1$ enters the excitation equation,

$$
\dot E_{fd}
=
\frac{1}{T'_{d0}}
\left(
-K_1E_{fd}
-K_2(V-V^\star)
-K_3W_1
+u
\right),
$$

the slow mode propagates through the chain

$$
W_1
\longrightarrow
E_{fd}
\longrightarrow
e
\longrightarrow
V.
$$

Therefore, the variables most affected by the slow pole are

$$
W_1,
\qquad
E_{fd},
\qquad
e,
\qquad
V.
$$

The rotor speed deviation $\omega$ is not the main slow variable. It is primarily shaped by the electromechanical pair

$$
-0.6933\pm j0.5644.
$$

Because the system is coupled, the slow PI pole can still leave a weak slow tail in $\omega$. However, the most visible slow tail usually appears in the voltage and excitation-related states.

The practical interpretation is:

> PI control improves steady-state voltage tracking, but the price is slower voltage and excitation recovery.

## 8. Transfer Function From $u$ to $\omega$

The transfer function from the excitation input $u$ to the frequency state $\omega$ is useful for root-locus analysis.

### 8.1 Without PI

The transfer function is

$$
G_{\omega u}^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}(s)
=
\frac{-147.39s}
{(s+63.05)(s+25.62)(s^2+1.393s+0.506)}.
$$

The zero at the origin means that a constant input perturbation in $u$ does not create a nonzero steady-state frequency deviation. Physically, the rotor speed must return to zero at a new equilibrium.

The poles of this transfer function are

$$
s=-63.05,
\qquad
s=-25.62,
\qquad
s=-0.6965\pm j0.1446.
$$

These are the same as the eigenvalues of the linearized no-PI state matrix.

### 8.2 With PI

With PI control, the transfer function becomes

$$
G_{\omega u}^{\mathrm{PI}}(s)
=
\frac{-118.15s^2}
{(s+62.45)(s+26.19)(s+0.03578)(s^2+1.387s+0.7991)}.
$$

The important new factor in the denominator is

$$
s+0.03578.
$$

This is the additional slow pole introduced by integral action.

The numerator contains $s^2$, which means this $u\to\omega$ channel has two zeros at the origin. Therefore, the slow integral mode may not dominate the frequency output as strongly as it dominates the voltage-related variables.

This agrees with the time-domain interpretation:

- $\omega$ is mainly affected by the electromechanical mode.
- $V$, $E_{fd}$, $e$, and $W_1$ are more visibly affected by the slow PI pole.

## 9. Root-Locus Interpretation for $u\to\omega$

The root locus of $u\to\omega$ shows how the closed-loop poles would move if a scalar feedback gain were placed around this linearized input-output channel.

For the no-PI model,

$$
G_{\omega u}^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}(s)
=
\frac{-147.39s}
{(s+63.05)(s+25.62)(s^2+1.393s+0.506)}.
$$

This transfer function has four open-loop poles and one zero at the origin.

For the PI model,

$$
G_{\omega u}^{\mathrm{PI}}(s)
=
\frac{-118.15s^2}
{(s+62.45)(s+26.19)(s+0.03578)(s^2+1.387s+0.7991)}.
$$

This transfer function has five open-loop poles and two zeros at the origin.

The most important root-locus difference is the extra pole near the origin:

$$
s=-0.03578.
$$

Because this pole is close to the imaginary axis, it indicates a slow mode. In a feedback design, this pole can strongly affect low-frequency behavior and the long-time transient.

However, because the $u\to\omega$ transfer function has zeros at the origin, the slow pole is not necessarily the most visible mode in the frequency output. To see the slow PI effect more clearly, it is useful to also inspect the channels

$$
u\to V,
\qquad
u\to E_{fd},
\qquad
u\to W_1.
$$

## 10. Numerical Comparison Summary

| Quantity | Without PI | With PI |
|---|---:|---:|
| $E_{fd,e}$ | 0.3611 | 0.7700 |
| $e_e$ | 0.6785 | 0.8464 |
| $\delta_e$ | 0.6489 | 0.5057 |
| $\omega_e$ | 0 | 0 |
| $W_{1,e}$ | not included | -2.3100 |
| $V_e$ | 0.8097 | 0.9000 |
| Voltage tracking | steady-state error | zero steady-state error |
| Extra slow pole | no | yes, $-0.0358$ |
| Electromechanical pair | $-0.6965\pm j0.1446$ | $-0.6933\pm j0.5644$ |
| Electromechanical damping ratio | 0.979 | 0.776 |

The most important voltage-regulation result is

$$
V_e^{\mathrm{no\ PI}}=0.8097\ne 0.9,
$$

whereas

$$
V_e^{\mathrm{PI}}=0.9.
$$

Thus, PI control successfully eliminates the terminal voltage steady-state error.

The most important small-signal-stability result is that the PI controller adds the slow pole

$$
-0.0358.
$$

This pole creates a slow convergence tail mainly in the excitation and voltage states.

## 11. Main Takeaways

1. A proportional AVR can stabilize the SMIB system, but it does not generally guarantee $V=V^\star$ at steady state.

2. Adding integral action introduces

   $$
   \dot W_1=V-V^\star,
   $$

   which forces

   $$
   V_e=V^\star
   $$

   at equilibrium.

3. The PI controller changes the operating point significantly:

   $$
   E_{fd,e}: 0.3611\to 0.7700,
   $$

   $$
   e_e: 0.6785\to 0.8464,
   $$

   $$
   \delta_e: 0.6489\to 0.5057.
   $$

4. The PI controller adds a slow pole:

   $$
   -0.0358.
   $$

5. This slow pole mainly affects the voltage and excitation channel:

   $$
   W_1,
   \qquad
   E_{fd},
   \qquad
   e,
   \qquad
   V.
   $$

6. The frequency state $\omega$ is still mainly shaped by the electromechanical complex pair, not by the slow integral pole.

7. Therefore, for this SMIB example, PI control improves steady-state voltage regulation, but it can make voltage and excitation recovery slower and can also change the damping properties of the electromechanical mode.
